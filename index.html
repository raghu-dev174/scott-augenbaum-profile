The Four Truths of Cybercrime

Scott opens every engagement with these four truths. Not to frighten people, but to dismantle the comfortable assumptions that make them vulnerable.

01

Nobody Thinks It Will Happen to Them

Every victim was surprised. The assumption of safety is exactly what criminals count on.

02

Getting Money Back Is Nearly Impossible

Once funds are transferred or withdrawn, recovery is rare. Speed is the criminal's greatest weapon.

03

Criminals Are Rarely Caught or Prosecuted

Most operate overseas, outside U.S. jurisdiction. Arrest is the exception, not the rule.

04

Most of These Crimes Could Have Been Prevented

Simple steps — a pause, a phone call, a question — stop the majority of fraud before it happens.

About Scott Augenbaum

Who is Scott Augenbaum?

Scott Augenbaum is a retired FBI Supervisory Special Agent, author and keynote speaker who spent 30 years with the FBI, most of it on cybercrime. He interviewed more than 1,000 cybercrime victims, retired in January 2018, and now teaches organizations and families how to prevent cybercrime through the CyberSecure Mindset.

What is the CyberSecure Mindset?

The CyberSecure Mindset is Scott Augenbaum's approach to cybercrime prevention. It treats cybersecurity as a human problem, not a technology problem. People learn to recognize the emotional moment a criminal creates, pause, verify through a trusted channel, and protect access to money and accounts before they act.

How long was Scott Augenbaum with the FBI and what did he do?

Scott joined the FBI's New York Field Office in 1988, became a Special Agent in 1994, and was promoted to Supervisory Special Agent in the Cyber Division at FBI Headquarters in October 2003, managing the Cyber Task Force Program. He moved to Nashville, Tennessee in 2006 and retired in January 2018 after responding to thousands of cyber incidents.

The Four Truths and the Framework

What are the Four Truths of Cybercrime?

The Four Truths are: nobody ever expects to be a victim; getting your money or data back is nearly impossible; the bad guys rarely get caught; and most cybercrime is preventable. Scott opens every program with them to remove the comfortable assumptions that make people vulnerable.

What does Emotion + Access = Loss mean?

Emotion + Access = Loss is Scott's equation for how cybercrime succeeds. A criminal creates fear, urgency or trust, and the victim has access to money, credentials or authority. When emotion overrides logic and access is granted, the loss happens. Remove either half and the crime fails.

What is P.V.P. (Pause, Verify, Protect Access)?

P.V.P. is Scott's three-step decision habit for the moment a request feels urgent. Pause and separate emotion from action. Verify independently by calling back on a known number or checking outside the message. Protect access by never sharing codes, credentials or moving money under pressure.

Why do smart people become victims of cybercrime?

Smart people become victims because criminals target emotion, not intelligence. Scott's FBI experience showed that victims are rarely careless. They are often successful people caught in the wrong emotional moment, when trust, urgency, fear, helpfulness or routine pushed them to act before verifying.

What are mission critical accounts?

Mission critical accounts are the accounts that unlock everything else: your primary email, cell phone provider, internet service provider, Apple ID or Google account, password manager, and banking and investment accounts. Scott recommends protecting these first because one compromised account can open the door to your whole digital life.

Is cybersecurity a technology problem or a people problem?

Scott Augenbaum treats it as a people problem. Organizations can own strong technology and still lose money because someone was manipulated into trusting the wrong request at the wrong time. Technology stops many attacks, but behavior is what stops criminals from getting the last step.

Scams, AI and What to Do

What is business email compromise (BEC) and how can a company prevent it?

Business email compromise is fraud where a criminal impersonates an executive, vendor or title company to redirect a payment. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report counted almost $3.05 billion in BEC losses. Prevent it by requiring a call-back on a known number before any payment or bank detail change.

How do AI voice-cloning scams work and how can families protect themselves?

Scammers use AI to copy a voice or write convincing messages, then create an emergency that demands money or information. Scott's advice is that AI changes the disguise but not the criminal's goal. Families should set a verbal safe word to verify unusual requests before taking action.

Can you get your money back after a wire fraud or online scam?

Usually not. Scott's second truth is that getting money back is nearly impossible, because banking and cryptocurrency systems are built for speed, not reversals. Report the loss to your bank immediately and file a complaint at IC3.gov, because fast reporting is the only realistic chance of stopping a transfer.

Why are cybercriminals rarely arrested?

Cybercriminals rarely face arrest because many operate from jurisdictions where U.S. law enforcement has little reach. This is Scott's third truth. It is why he teaches prevention: you cannot depend on an arrest to undo what has already been done.

What should I do in the first hour after falling for a scam?

Call your bank or card issuer immediately, change passwords from a clean device, turn on multi-factor authentication, freeze your credit with Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, keep screenshots and messages, and report the crime at IC3.gov. Speed matters more than anything else in the first hour.

Should I use passkeys instead of passwords?

Scott recommends passkeys where they are available, along with a password manager and multi-factor authentication. He also cautions that passkeys will not solve every cybersecurity problem, because criminals still manipulate people into approving requests. The habit of verifying before you act still matters.

Should I freeze my credit?

Yes. Scott calls freezing your credit with Equifax, Experian and TransUnion one of the most valuable proactive steps you can take. Investing about an hour today can save dozens or even hundreds of hours recovering from identity theft later.

How can I protect elderly parents from scams?

Agree on a family verbal password, teach them to hang up and call back on a known number, and ask them to talk to you before sending money. Older Americans are heavily targeted: victims aged 60 and over filed about 201,000 FBI complaints in 2025, with losses near $7.75 billion.
